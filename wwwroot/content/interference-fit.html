How an interference fit is calculated

An interference fit holds by friction alone. The shaft is made slightly larger than the bore, so assembling the two puts the hub in tension and the shaft in compression. That contact pressure, multiplied by the friction coefficient and the contact area, is the entire load capacity of the joint — there is no key, no pin, nothing else carrying the torque.

The chain from tolerance to torque runs in four steps. Interference from the fit is the shaft deviation minus the hub deviation; a Ø50 H7/s6 fit gives 59 µm at most and 18 µm at least. Roughness loss removes part of it before the joint sees any load, because the surface peaks flatten during assembly. What survives produces the contact pressure, and the pressure sets the transmissible torque. The sections below take each of those in turn.

The equations

Contact pressure follows Lamé's thick-cylinder solution, which is what DIN 7190 applies to a cylindrical joint:

p = δ_eff / [ d · (C₁/E₁ + C₂/E₂) ]

C_1 = (1 + Qi^2)/(1 - Qi^2) - nu_1     Qi = d_i / d    (shaft bore ratio)
C_2 = (1 + Qa^2)/(1 - Qa^2) + nu_2     Qa = d / d_a    (hub diameter ratio)

T     = mu . p . pi . d^2 . L / 2
F_a   = mu . p . pi . d . L
F_t   = 2000 . T . phi / d
F_res = sqrt(F_t^2 + F_a,applied^2)   against   mu . p_min . pi . d . L

The roughness allowance is 0,8 (Rzshaft + Rzhub), taken off the geometric interference before any pressure is worked out.

Torque and axial force share one capacity. There is a single friction interface, so the two load components are not independent: the resultant Fres is what has to stay inside µ·pmin·π·d·L. Two separate safety factors of 1,28 and 1,59 on the same joint can mean a resultant safety of 0,90.

Stresses are read where they peak, which is not always the fit surface. In a hollow shaft the largest stress sits at the bore, where the radial stress has relaxed to zero:

shaft, fit surface:  sigma_r = -p            sigma_t = -p (1 + Qi^2)/(1 - Qi^2)
shaft, bore:         sigma_r = 0             sigma_t = -2 p / (1 - Qi^2)
hub, bore:           sigma_r = -p            sigma_t = +p (1 + Qa^2)/(1 - Qa^2)
hub, outer surface:  sigma_r = 0             sigma_t = +2 p Qa^2 / (1 - Qa^2)

sigma_v = sqrt(sigma_t^2 - sigma_t sigma_r + sigma_r^2)      (plane stress)

At Qi = 0,8 the bore value is a third higher than the one at the fit surface. A rotating part also carries a centrifugal hoop stress on top of this, and that term is added at every point.

Where the inputs come from

Tolerances. Pick a fit rather than typing microns. H7/s6 and H7/u6 cover most shaft–hub work; H7/r6 is lighter. The calculator fills the deviations from ISO 286 once you choose the fit and the diameter.

Roughness. Use the Rz you will actually machine to. Turned surfaces land around Rz 10–25 µm, ground surfaces around Rz 4 µm. This matters more than most people expect: on a Ø50 joint, going from ground to turned can remove a third of the interference.

Friction. 0,10–0,15 is the usual band for dry steel on steel. Take the low end unless the surfaces are degreased and you can prove it. Oil-injection assembly reduces it further.

Hub outside diameter. A thin hub yields before it grips. The ratio d/dₐ drives the whole calculation, which is why the field is not optional.

Worked example

A C45 gear hub on a C45 shaft, Ø50 H7/s6, 60 mm long, hub outside diameter 90 mm, both surfaces at Rz 6,3 µm, µ = 0,12, carrying 250 Nm.

Interference from the fit          59,00 /  18,00 µm   (max / min)
Roughness loss  0,8 (6,3 + 6,3)    10,08 µm
Effective interference             48,92 /   7,92 µm

Contact pressure                   69,67 /  11,28 MPa
Transmissible torque             1969,9  /  318,9  Nm
Hub von Mises stress (bore)       177,3  MPa   (C45 yield 430 -> 2,43)
Shaft von Mises stress            69,7   MPa   (solid shaft -> 6,17)

Safety against slipping             1,28        (318,9 / 250)
Assembly force, press fit          98,5  kN
Hub heating for shrink fit         149    deg C

Notice the spread. At maximum material the joint would carry 1970 Nm — eight times the design load. At minimum material it carries 319 Nm. The minimum is the only number that decides whether the joint slips, and it is the one a quick hand calculation usually skips.

Push the same joint to 1200 Nm and the minimum-material safety factor falls to 0,27. The fit would hold on most of the parts produced and let go on the rest, which is the worst kind of failure to diagnose.

Add a 10 kN axial force to the 250 Nm and nothing about the pressure changes, but the governing check does. The torque alone gives 1,28 and the axial force alone 1,59; their resultant is 14,1 kN against a capacity of 12,8 kN, so the joint slips at a resultant safety of 0,90. Neither separate figure shows that.

Bore the shaft out to Ø40 (Qi = 0,8) and the pressure falls to 31,3 MPa, but the shaft now governs its own yield check: the stress at its bore is 173,7 MPa against 129,6 MPa at the fit surface, so the safety factor is 2,48 rather than the 3,32 the fit surface alone would suggest.

It is the hub that is heated for a shrink fit, not the shaft. The shaft cooling temperature on the same card is the alternative, not an addition.

Reading the result

Four checks have to pass, and they pull in opposite directions.

Slipping, at minimum interference. Aim for 1,3–1,5. Read the resultant row when both a torque and an axial force act — it is the governing one, and it is lower than either load taken alone. This is the check that decides the design almost every time.

Yield, at maximum interference and in the worst operating state. On a solid shaft in a reasonably thick hub it is the hub that yields. Give it a factor of about 1,5 on von Mises. Two things are easy to get wrong here: a hollow shaft is stressed hardest at its bore, not at the fit surface, and rotation or a hotter hub both relieve the interference — so the state that loads the parts hardest is usually standstill at room temperature, which is where they sit after assembly and at every shutdown. The results name the state and the point each figure was read at.

Assembly force, if pressing — 100 kN needs a real press — and assembly temperature, if shrinking. Above roughly 200–250 °C a hardened or tempered steel starts losing its heat treatment.

Making the fit tighter fixes the first and worsens the second. Making the hub thicker improves both, and is usually the cheapest change available.

Frequently asked questions

Does the calculator account for temperature and speed?

Yes. Enter the service temperatures and the interference is corrected for differential expansion — this matters as soon as the two parts are different materials. Rotation is included as a centrifugal loss: both parts grow outwards, the hub bore more than the shaft, and the difference comes off the interference. Note the direction: both of those effects reduce the pressure, so the yield check is run at standstill and at room temperature as well, and the worst of the states is the one reported. Rotation also adds a centrifugal hoop stress, which is included.

Why does the safety factor read 0,00 with no explanation?

It should not any more. When the effective interference reaches zero the results say which input consumed it — a transition fit that has clearance at its loose limit, a surface finish rough enough to eat the whole allowance, service temperatures that open the joint, or centrifugal expansion at speed.

Why does my joint fail at minimum interference but pass at maximum?

Because both are real parts. Tolerances mean some of your production sits at each end. Design to the minimum.

Can I use a hollow shaft?

Yes — enter the bore. A hollow shaft is more compliant, so the same interference produces less pressure, but it is stressed hardest at its bore rather than at the fit surface. The calculator evaluates both points and reports the larger, naming which one it used.

What is not covered?

Elastic–plastic design, which DIN 7190 permits, is not implemented — every result here assumes both parts stay elastic. Neither is the pressure peak at the ends of the hub (edge effect), nor a hub shorter or longer than the fit length. The assembly clearance assumed for the shrink-fit temperature is shown on the results card so you can judge it; it is a practice value, not a figure from the standard.

Which standard is this?

DIN 7190, using the Lamé thick-cylinder solution. Tolerance deviations come from ISO 286.
