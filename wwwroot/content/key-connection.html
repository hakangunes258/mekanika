How a parallel key connection is calculated

A parallel key is a form-fit connection: the key sits in a slot in the shaft and a slot in the hub, and torque passes through the two flanks that press against each other. Nothing relies on friction. That makes the calculation short, but it also means the joint fails by crushing the sides of the keyway rather than by slipping quietly — a key connection that is overloaded usually takes the hub with it.

Three checks decide the design, and only one of them is usually critical. Surface pressure on the shaft keyway and surface pressure on the hub keyway compare the flank load against what the material can take without denting; the hub is almost always the weaker of the two because it bears on less depth. Shear across the key is checked as well, but on standard DIN 6885 keys it is rarely the governing mode.

The equations

The torque becomes a tangential force at the shaft surface, and that force is spread over the bearing area of each keyway:

F_t     = 2000 T φ / d             tangential force at the shaft radius
F_key   = F_t / (0,75 n)            n > 1; a single key carries F_t

l_eff   = l − b                     round-ended key: the ends do not bear
l_tr    = min(l_eff, 1,3 d)         length credited with carrying load
A_shaft = l_tr · t₁                shaft bears on the keyway depth
A_hub   = l_tr · (h − t₁)          hub bears on what is left of the key

p       = F_key / A                 τ = F_key / (l_tr · b)

p_allow = 0,9 · min(R_e,part, R_e,key)
τ_allow = 0,6 · R_e,key

Why the key material appears in the pressure check. Both flanks of a contact pair are loaded by the same pressure — the keyway flank and the side of the key itself — so the weaker of the two materials is what decides. A soft key in a hard hub crushes on the key, not on the hub, and the calculator names which part supplied the allowable next to the value.

Why the length is capped at 1,3 d. The shaft twists along the length of the key, so the far end of a long key stops taking its share of the load. Past about 1,3× the shaft diameter the extra length is not credited; the pressures are computed on l_tr and the results say when the cap bit.

With more than one key the load does not divide evenly — manufacturing differences mean one key takes more than its share. Each key is credited at 0,75, so two keys carry 1,5 times the load of one and three carry 2,25 times, not 2 and 3.

Where the inputs come from

Key size. Do not choose it. DIN 6885 fixes b, h, t₁ and t₂ from the shaft diameter, and the calculator suggests the right key as soon as you enter the diameter — press Apply to fill all four. A non-standard key means a non-standard cutter and a non-standard hub. The suggestion covers shafts up to 130 mm; above that, take the dimensions from the standard itself.

Key length. This is the real design variable, and the only one you get to pick freely. It has to be longer than the key is wide — a round-ended key bears on l − b, so l = b is a key with no bearing length at all — and there is little point going past about 1,3× the shaft diameter, because the shaft twists along the key and the far end stops carrying load. The calculator refuses the first and caps the second rather than quietly rating length that does nothing.

Keyway depths. t₁ must be less than the key height: the key has to stand h − t₁ proud of the shaft to reach the hub at all, and that protrusion — not t₂ — is what bears on the hub. t₂ is cut slightly deeper on purpose, so the key seats on its flanks rather than on its top face.

Load factor. Use 1,0 only for a genuinely steady drive. An electric motor into a pump is around 1,25; anything with reversal or impact needs 1,75 to 2,0. This factor multiplies straight into the pressure, so it moves the result more than any other single input. Note what it is: a static allowance. With a reversing load the real risks are fatigue and the keyway hammering itself oversize, and neither is captured by a single multiplier.

Materials. Enter all three — the key included. The hub is usually the softest part and therefore the one that decides the answer, but a mild steel key in a hardened hub makes the key the governing part instead.

Worked example

A Ø40 shaft, the DIN 6885 key for that size (12 × 8, t₁ = 5,0, t₂ = 3,3), 50 mm long, one key, steady load, C45 throughout, carrying 500 Nm.

Effective length     50 − 12       38,0 mm
Load-carrying length min(38; 52)    38,0 mm   1,3 d = 52, so not capped
Contact area, shaft  38 × 5,0      190,0 mm²
Contact area, hub    38 × 3,0      114,0 mm²

Tangential force  2·500·1000/40    25 000 N

Surface pressure, shaft    131,6 MPa   allow 387,0   → SF 2,94
Surface pressure, hub      219,3 MPa   allow 387,0   → SF 1,76
Shear stress in key         54,8 MPa   allow 258,0   → SF 4,71

Required key length, for SF 1,5       45 mm
Provided                              50 mm

Look at the two pressures. The hub carries the same force on 114 mm² where the shaft has 190 mm², so its pressure is two-thirds higher and its safety factor is the one that matters. The hub keyway is nearly always the governing check, because the key stands only h − t₁ = 3 mm proud of the shaft while it sits 5 mm deep in it.

Shear is not close — 4,71 against 1,76. That is typical: on a standard key the flanks crush long before the key shears, which is why a key shear calculation on its own gives a dangerously optimistic answer.

Change nothing but the key material, from C45 to S235JR, and the allowable pressure falls from 387,0 to 211,5 MPa, because the key is now the weaker side of both contacts. The hub factor goes from 1,76 to 0,96 — the same joint, failing, on a part most people would not think to check.

Reading the result

Aim for 1,5 on the lowest of the three factors, and expect that to be the hub. Below 1,0 the keyway flank yields; the key does not break, it beds into the hub, the fit becomes sloppy, and then it hammers.

Required key length is the shortest key that reaches 1,5 on all three checks, rounded up to the next 5 mm. It is sized for the same figure the status column judges by, so fitting it lands you in the green rather than one step short of it. DIN 6885 has its own length series (… 28, 32, 36, 40, 45, 50, 56 …) — round up to a standard length from there.

If the required length comes out past 1,3× the shaft diameter, lengthening the key is not the fix, and the calculator says so instead of recommending a key that cannot work. Use two keys at 180°, a larger shaft, harder parts, or a friction-based joint such as an interference or clamp connection.

Two keys do not double the capacity. Each key is credited at 0,75, so two carry 1,5 times the load of one. Three are offered but not recommended: the load cannot be shared that evenly in practice, and the 2,25 the calculator credits is optimistic.

The allowable values are shown next to the stresses, with the part that supplied each one named. If a safety factor looks wrong, that row is where to look first — particularly on a cast iron hub, where 0,9 · Re is a rough stand-in for a material that has no real yield point.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the hub pressure higher than the shaft pressure?

Because the key is buried deeper in the shaft than it stands proud of it. On a 12 × 8 key, 5 mm is in the shaft and only 3 mm is available to bear on the hub.

Should I use the total length or the effective length?

Effective. A round-ended key does not bear on its rounded ends, so l − b is what carries load. The calculator does this for you, and then caps the result at 1,3 d.

Why does a longer key stop improving the result?

Because the shaft twists along the key. The end nearest the load takes far more than its share, and past roughly 1,3× the shaft diameter the far end contributes nothing. A 100 mm key on a 40 mm shaft is rated on 52 mm of bearing length here, not on all 88.

Does the key also centre the hub?

No. A parallel key transmits torque only. Centring is the job of the shaft-hub fit, which is why keyed hubs are normally mounted on a transition or interference fit as well.

Is the shaft itself checked?

No. The keyway is a stress raiser, and the shaft needs its own torsion and bending check with that notch effect included. This calculator sizes the connection, not the shaft.

Which standard is this?

DIN 6885 for the key dimensions and keyway depths; the surface pressure and shear checks follow standard machine-design practice. The allowable pressures are 0,9 · Re rather than the hardness- and support-factor route of DIN 6892.
