How ball bearing life is calculated

Bearing life is a statistical statement, not a prediction. The basic rating life L10 is the number of revolutions that 90 % of a large group of identical bearings will reach before the first sign of fatigue on a raceway. One bearing in ten is expected to fail sooner — that is what the subscript means, and it is why two identical machines can differ by a factor of several in service life without anything being wrong.

The calculation itself is short. Combine the radial and axial loads into one equivalent radial load P, divide the bearing's dynamic rating C by it, and cube the result. The work is almost entirely in finding P, because the factors X and Y that weight the axial load depend on the axial load itself through the ratio f₀Fa/C₀.

The equations

ISO 281 gives the life directly from the load ratio, with the exponent set by the rolling element shape:

P = fᶠ (X Fₕ + Y Fₐ)              equivalent dynamic load
                                 X = 1, Y = 0 when Fₐ/Fₕ ≤ e

L₁₀  = (C / P)ᵧ                    p = 3 for ball bearings
L₁₀ₕ = 10⁶ L₁₀ / (60 n)              life in hours

S₀ = C₀ / P₀                       static safety

The limit e is interpolated from the ISO 281 table using f₀Fa/C₀. Below it the axial load contributes nothing — the bearing carries it in the existing contact — and above it both X and Y change.

Where the inputs come from

The bearing. Choose it from the list and C, C₀ and f₀ fill in from the catalogue. If you are working from a manufacturer's data sheet, type the three values instead — ratings differ between makers for the same designation, sometimes by 20 %.

Loads. These are the loads on this bearing, not on the shaft. Working them out from the shaft reactions is usually the harder half of the job and this calculator does not do it for you.

Machine factor fw. A blunt allowance for shock that the nominal loads do not contain. 1,0 only for genuinely smooth running; 1,5 for a reciprocating machine; 2,0 and above for crushers and impact. It multiplies P directly, so it cubes into the life — fw = 1,5 costs you two thirds of the calculated hours.

Reliability. 90 % is the default and is what L10 means. Selecting 99 % applies a₁ = 0,25, i.e. you must accept a quarter of the life to have a one-in-a-hundred failure rate instead of one in ten.

Worked example

A 6205 deep groove ball bearing (25 × 52 × 15, C = 14,0 kN, C₀ = 7,9 kN, f₀ = 13,9) carrying 2000 N radial and 400 N axial at 1500 rpm, smooth running, 90 % reliability.

f₀ Fₐ / C₀   13,9 · 400 / 7900        0,704
e interpolated from ISO 281              0,261
Fₐ / Fₕ      400 / 2000                 0,200   ≤ e

X = 1,00   Y = 0,00
Equivalent dynamic load  P              2000 N
Load ratio  C / P                        7,00

Basic rating life  L₁₀                343,00 × 10⁶ rev
                   L₁₀ₕ                  3811 hours

Static equivalent load  P₀             2,00 kN
Static safety  S₀                       3,95   ✓

The axial load disappears from the answer entirely. At 0,200 the ratio Fa/Fr sits below e = 0,261, so Y = 0 and P equals the radial load alone. This is not the calculator ignoring the axial load — a deep groove bearing genuinely carries a modest thrust within its existing contact ellipse without adding to the fatigue load.

Now watch the sensitivity. Raise the radial load from 2000 N to 2500 N — 25 % — and the life falls from 3811 hours to 1951, very nearly half. The cube law means every load estimate you make is amplified threefold in the answer, which is worth remembering before spending effort on the third significant figure of anything else.

Reading the result

Compare L10h against a target for the machine class. Roughly: 4000–8000 h for intermittent industrial use, 20 000–30 000 h for eight-hour-a-day production machinery, 50 000 h and up for continuous plant. A calculated 3811 hours is short for anything running daily.

Static safety S₀ is a separate question and is checked against the worst load the bearing sees standing still or turning slowly, including shock. Below about 1,5 the raceway takes permanent indentation, and the bearing is then noisy for the rest of its life regardless of what the fatigue calculation said.

The adjusted life here applies the reliability factor a₁ only. The full ISO 281:2007 modifier aISO, which accounts for lubrication film, contamination and the fatigue load limit, is not included. In clean, well-lubricated conditions aISO can exceed 1 substantially; with poor lubrication or dirt it falls well below. Treat the figure as a catalogue-style estimate, which is what the note on the results says.

Frequently asked questions

Why did my axial load make no difference?

Because Fa/Fr came out at or below e. That is normal for a deep groove bearing with a light thrust. Increase the axial load and you will see X and Y change.

Is L10h how long my bearing will last?

No. It is the life 90 % of a population reaches. Median life is roughly five times L10, and one bearing in ten falls short of it. Neither number tells you about a bearing that fails from dirt, misalignment or a bad fit — which in practice is most of them.

Why do two catalogues give different C for the same bearing?

Because C depends on internal geometry and steel quality, which differ between manufacturers. Use the rating from the maker you will actually buy.

Which standard is this?

ISO 281 for the rating life and the X/Y factors, ISO 76 for static load rating.
