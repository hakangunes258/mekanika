How a torsion spring is calculated

A torsion spring stores energy in bending, not in torsion — the name describes what the spring does, not what the wire does. Twisting the legs about the coil axis bends the wire along its length, so the governing stress is a bending stress and the governing modulus is E, not G. Every formula on this page differs from the compression spring page for that one reason.

The second thing that separates a torsion spring from the others is that its diameter changes as it deflects. Wound in the direction of the load, the coils tighten and the bore shrinks. A torsion spring running on a pin that fitted at rest can seize on it at full deflection, and no stress calculation will warn you — which is why the results carry a table of inner diameter against deflection.

The equations

Bending of the wire, with a curvature correction for the inner fibre:

Rᵐ = E d⁴ / (3667 Dₘ nᵉ)              rate, Nmm per degree

M = Rᵐ · α                             torque at deflection α

σ = q · 32 M / (π d³)                 bending stress
q = (4w² − w − 1) / (4w² − 4w)         correction factor, index w

Dᵢ = Dᵢ₀ · n / (n + α/360)             bore shrinks as it winds up

ne is the equivalent coil count, which includes the compliance of the legs. Long legs make the spring softer than the coil count alone suggests, and the calculator accounts for it — here 8 active coils behave like 8,42.

Where the inputs come from

Winding direction. Get this right and load the spring in the closing direction. A torsion spring loaded so that it unwinds opens its coils, loses the benefit of the correction factor, and comes off its pin.

Leg length and type. The legs are part of the spring, not fittings on the end of it. They add compliance, which lowers the rate, and tangent legs also carry their own bending stress at the root where they leave the coil.

The two deflection angles. Position 1 is usually the pre-loaded rest position and position 2 the fully worked one. A torsion spring is almost always installed with some preload, so entering 0° for position 1 rarely describes the real installation.

Allowable stress is 0,70 Rm for a torsion spring, against 0,45 Rm for a compression spring. That is not an inconsistency — bending allows a higher fraction of tensile strength than shear does.

Worked example

2,5 mm EN 10270-1 SH wire, 20 mm mean coil diameter, 8 active coils, right-hand wound, 40 mm tangent legs, working between 30° and 90°.

Spring rate                           13,024 Nmm/°

Position 1   30°    M₁   390,7 Nmm  (0,391 Nm)
Position 2   90°    M₂  1172,2 Nmm  (1,172 Nm)

Bending stress at 30°                   281 MPa   → SF 4,24  ✓
Bending stress at 90°                   843 MPa   → SF 1,41  ⚠
Allowable  0,70 × Rᵐ                  1190 MPa

Inner diameter, free                   17,50 mm
Inner diameter at 90°                  16,97 mm
Inner diameter at maximum              16,76 mm

Follow the bore. It starts at 17,50 mm and closes to 16,97 mm at the working angle — over half a millimetre. A 17 mm pin would fit the spring at rest and be gripped by it at full deflection, adding friction, wear and an unpredictable contribution to the torque. Size the pin against the smallest diameter in the table, not the free one.

The safety factor is 1,41 at the working angle and 4,24 at rest — the same spring, a factor of three apart, because stress rises linearly with angle. There is not much room left here: another 20° of over-travel and this spring is at its allowable stress. That is worth knowing before someone specifies a 120° mechanism for it.

Reading the result

Check the safety factor at the largest angle, which is the one that governs. 1,5 is a reasonable target for a static spring; less than that leaves no allowance for over-travel, and over-travel is what mechanisms do.

Check the inner diameter against your pin or shaft at maximum deflection, with clearance. This is the check that is unique to torsion springs and the one most often skipped.

Watch the maximum allowable deflection. The results give the angle at which the spring reaches its allowable stress. Design the mechanism so it cannot physically exceed it — a hard stop is cheaper than a broken spring.

The legs matter. Long legs soften the spring, and a leg that is loaded as a lever also bends at its root. If leg deflection is part of your mechanism, the rate you get will be lower than a coil-count calculation predicts.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the allowable stress higher than for a compression spring?

Because a torsion spring is loaded in bending and a compression spring in shear. Steel tolerates a higher fraction of its tensile strength in bending — 0,70 Rm against 0,45.

Which way should the spring be wound?

So that the load winds it tighter. Loading a torsion spring in the opening direction reduces its capacity and makes it unstable on its pin.

Does the coil diameter really change that much?

Yes. It scales as n/(n + α/360), so few coils and a large angle give the biggest change. Always check the pin fit at maximum deflection.

Which standard is this?

EN 13906-3 for the design method, with wire grades from EN 10270.
