How a torsion spring is calculated

A torsion spring stores energy in bending, not in torsion — the name describes what the spring does, not what the wire does. Twisting the legs about the coil axis bends the wire along its length, so the governing stress is a bending stress and the governing modulus is E, not G. Every formula on this page differs from the compression spring page for that one reason.

The second thing that separates a torsion spring from the others is that its diameter changes as it deflects. Wound in the direction of the load, the coils tighten and the bore shrinks. A torsion spring running on a pin that fitted at rest can seize on it at full deflection, and no stress calculation will warn you — which is why the results carry a table of inner diameter against deflection.

The equations

Bending of the wire, with a curvature correction for the inner fibre:

Rᵐ = E d⁴ / (3667 Dₘ nᵉ)              rate, Nmm per degree

M = Rᵐ · α                             torque at deflection α

sigma = q . 32 M / (pi d^3)             bending stress
q     = (w + 0,07) / (w - 0,75)         EN 13906-3 correction, index w

D_i(alpha) = D_m . n/(n +/- alpha/360) - d     bore under load
                   + closing load, - opening load

The bore formula scales the MEAN diameter and then subtracts the wire. Scaling the inner diameter instead carries the wire thickness along with the coil, which reads high — 0,08 mm on this spring, 0,50 mm at n = 2 — in exactly the place it matters, because the mandrel is sized against the smallest bore.

The sign follows the LOAD direction, not the winding hand. Either hand can be loaded either way. A load that winds the coil tighter shrinks the bore onto the mandrel; one that unwinds it grows the bore and works against the residual stresses left by coiling, so the permissible stress has to come down.

ne is the equivalent coil count, which includes the compliance of the legs. Long legs make the spring softer than the coil count alone suggests, and the calculator accounts for it — here 8 active coils behave like 8,42.

Where the inputs come from

Winding direction and load direction are two different things. The hand of the winding is drawing and manufacturing information; either hand can be loaded either way, so it does not on its own decide whether the coil tightens or opens. The load direction is the one that changes the result, and it is a separate input. Load the spring in the closing direction where you can: a spring that unwinds under load grows its bore away from the pin, and the residual stresses from coiling then work against it rather than for it, so the permissible stress has to be reduced.

Mandrel diameter. Optional, but without it the clearance check cannot run at all — there is nothing to compare the bore against. Manufacturers size the pin at about 0,85 of the smallest bore, and the results give that figure.

Leg length and type. The legs are part of the spring, not fittings on the end of it. They add compliance, which lowers the rate, and tangent legs also carry their own bending stress at the root where they leave the coil.

The two deflection angles. Position 1 is usually the pre-loaded rest position and position 2 the fully worked one. A torsion spring is almost always installed with some preload, so entering 0° for position 1 rarely describes the real installation.

Allowable stress is 0,70 Rm for a torsion spring, against 0,45 Rm for a compression spring. That is not an inconsistency — bending allows a higher fraction of tensile strength than shear does.

Worked example

2,5 mm EN 10270-1 SH wire, 20 mm mean coil diameter, 8 active coils, right-hand wound, 40 mm tangent legs, working between 30° and 90°.

Follow the bore. It starts at 17,50 mm and closes to 16,89 mm at the working angle, 16,66 mm at the permissible one. A 17 mm pin would fit the spring at rest and be gripped by it under load, adding friction, wear and an unpredictable contribution to the torque. Size the pin against the smallest diameter, not the free one — and enter it, or the clearance cannot be checked at all. The row used to show the bore and call it "adequate clearance" with no pin to compare it against.

The safety factor is 1,40 at the working angle and 4,19 at rest — the same spring, a factor of three apart, because stress rises linearly with angle. The permissible angle is 125,9°, so 90° uses 71 % of it: there is about 36° of over-travel in hand. That is worth knowing before someone specifies a 120° mechanism for it.

Reading the result

Check the safety factor at the largest angle, which is the one that governs. 1,5 is a reasonable target for a static spring; less than that leaves no allowance for over-travel, and over-travel is what mechanisms do.

Check the inner diameter against your pin or shaft at maximum deflection, with clearance. This is the check that is unique to torsion springs and the one most often skipped. Enter the mandrel diameter and the results do it for you, against the smallest bore over every position asked for — which is not always the "at maximum" row, because past the permissible angle that row stops being the smallest.

Watch the maximum allowable deflection. The results give the angle at which the spring reaches its allowable stress. Design the mechanism so it cannot physically exceed it — a hard stop is cheaper than a broken spring.

The legs matter. Long legs soften the spring: the equivalent coil count ne includes their compliance, so 8 active coils here behave like 8,42. If a leg length is left blank the calculator substitutes Dm rather than zero — a torsion spring has to have legs — and writes the value back into the field so you can see and change it.

What is not covered. The bending stress at the root of a tangent leg, where the leg leaves the coil, is not calculated: it depends on the bend radius there, which is not an input. On a spring whose legs are long relative to the coil that root can govern, so check it separately. The leg type also does not change the rate here, only the moment arm.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the allowable stress higher than for a compression spring?

Because a torsion spring is loaded in bending and a compression spring in shear. Steel tolerates a higher fraction of its tensile strength in bending — 0,70 Rm against 0,45.

Which way should the spring be wound?

So that the load winds it tighter. Loading a torsion spring in the opening direction reduces its capacity and makes it unstable on its pin.

Does the coil diameter really change that much?

Yes. It scales as n/(n + α/360), so few coils and a large angle give the biggest change. Always check the pin fit at maximum deflection.

Which standard is this?

EN 13906-3 for the design method, with wire grades from EN 10270.
