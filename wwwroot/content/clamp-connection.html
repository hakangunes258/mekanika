How a clamp connection is calculated

A clamp connection grips a shaft by squeezing a split hub around it. The bolts do not carry the torque — they generate a normal force at the bore, and friction against that normal force is what carries the torque. Everything in the calculation therefore runs in one direction: tightening torque to bolt preload, preload to contact pressure, pressure to friction capacity.

That chain has an unforgiving link at the start. Bolt preload from a tightening torque is only as repeatable as the thread friction, and a factor of two in achieved preload between a dry and an oiled thread is ordinary. A clamp joint sized with no margin on paper is a clamp joint that slips in service, which is why the sliding safety factor here wants to be higher than you would accept on a form-fit connection.

The equations

Preload from torque, then pressure from preload, then torque capacity from friction:

F_V = M_A / [ P/(2 pi) + mu_G d_2/(2 cos 30) + mu_K d_m/2 ]   bolt preload

sigma_red = F_V/A_s . sqrt(1 + 3 k^2)                         assembly stress
  k = 1,5 (d_2/d_S) (P/(pi d_2) + 1,155 mu_G)                 torsion coupling
F_M,zul = 0,9 R_p0,2 A_s / sqrt(1 + 3 k^2)                    usable preload

beta = 360 - 2 asin(b/d)                                      wrap angle, deg
l_F  = 0,85 L                                                 effective length

p     = n F_V / (d . l_F)                                     mean pressure
p_max = (pi/2) . p                                            peak pressure
F_N   = pi . n . F_V . (beta/360)                             normal force

T_cap = mu . F_N . d/2                                        torque capacity
F_a,cap = mu . F_N                                            axial capacity
F_N,req = sqrt(F_t^2 + F_a^2) / mu                            combined demand

Where the pressure relation comes from. Cut the clamp along the diametral joint plane. Every one of the n bolts crosses that plane, so the total preload has to balance the resultant of the bore pressure over one half shell: n·FV = ∫p·sinθ·(d/2)·lF dθ = p·d·lF. The mean pressure therefore follows from the projected area d·lF, while the normal force integrated over the wrapped surface is π times larger. The two are not the same area and using one where the other belongs is a factor of π either way.

The split removes part of the circumference, so the contact does not run the full 360°. lF = 0,85·L allows for the pressure falling off at the ends of the bore; it is a practice value, not a figure from a standard.

Where the inputs come from

Tightening torque. Take it from the bolt table for the class and thread friction you will actually have, not from the number stamped on the wrench in the drawer. The calculator reports the resulting bolt utilisation so you can see whether the figure you entered is realistic for the bolt.

Friction coefficient. 0,15 for dry steel on steel is the default and is already slightly optimistic for a machined bore. Drop to 0,10 if there is any chance of oil. Do not use published figures for clean laboratory surfaces.

Clamp outside diameter. A thin clamp bends instead of gripping, and the pressure concentrates opposite the slit. The ratio d/Dₐ drives the stress result.

Slit width. Wide enough that the two halves can actually close — if the slit shuts before the bolts are tight, the joint stops gaining pressure and the calculation no longer applies.

Number of bolts. Two is standard. One bolt makes the pressure distribution much more uneven than this model assumes; see the note in "Reading the result".

Worked example

A C45 clamp on a Ø40 shaft: outside diameter 70 mm, 50 mm long, 4 mm slit, two M8 class 8.8 bolts at 25 Nm each, µ = 0,15, carrying 200 Nm.

Effective contact angle           348,5°
Effective contact length           42,5 mm
Contact area                       5170 mm²

Preload per bolt                  18 262 N
Tensile stress                       499 MPa
Torsion factor (VDI 2230)          1,132
Assembly equivalent stress           565 MPa   (88 % of bolt yield)
Allowable preload F_M,zul         18 623 N     at M_A,zul = 25,5 Nm

Normal force at the bore         111 086 N
Mean contact pressure              21,5 MPa
Maximum contact pressure           33,7 MPa

Torque capacity                   333,3 Nm
Applied torque                    200,0 Nm
Safety against sliding              1,67
Hub von Mises stress (at p_max)    88,3 MPa   -> SF 4,87
Bolt yield safety                   1,13   governing

The joint holds, but read the two low numbers together. Sliding safety is 1,67 — acceptable — and it is bought by running the bolts at 88 % of yield, which leaves a bolt safety of only 1,13. Note where that 88 % comes from: the tensile stress alone is 499 MPa, 78 % of yield, and it is the torsion from the thread friction that takes the equivalent stress to 565 MPa. A reader who looked only at the tension would think there was 22 % in hand when there is 12 %.

25 Nm is also within 2 % of this bolt's allowable tightening torque, so there is no room to tighten for more grip even in principle. There is no spare capacity in either direction: the bolts are already the limiting part.

The hub is loafing at a safety of 4,87. That is the signature of a clamp joint that is bolt-limited rather than hub-limited, and it points straight at the fix — go to M10, or to four bolts, before making the clamp any heavier.

Reading the result

Sliding safety should be 1,5 to 2,0, higher than you would demand elsewhere, because the preload behind it is uncertain. A slipping clamp gives no warning and often destroys the shaft surface on its way round.

Bolt yield safety is the other half of the same trade. Torque capacity and bolt stress rise together, so improving one worsens the other; the only way out is more bolts or bigger ones. It is read on the assembly equivalent stress, which includes the torsion the thread friction leaves in the bolt — about 13 % above the tensile stress for an M8 at µG = 0,12. The results also give FM,zul and the torque that produces it; enter more than that and the capacities are computed at FM,zul rather than at a preload the bolt cannot deliver, and the page says so.

Contact pressure matters for soft shafts and for hollow ones. The maximum figure, not the mean, is what to compare against the permissible pressure — and it is also what drives the hub stress check.

Grey cast iron is a special case. It has no yield point, so the "yield strength" used for it is a stand-in and von Mises is the wrong criterion: a brittle material fails on maximum normal stress against Rm. The results say so when either part is a lamellar iron. Nodular iron (EN-GJS / GGG) does have a proof stress and is unaffected.

Follow the governing check when you change something. The results name it. Tightening harder helps a sliding-limited joint and makes a bolt-limited one worse, which is the most common way to get this backwards.

This module is not verified against a reference calculation. It models the clamp as a split hub with a reduced contact arc. A single-slit clamp also works as a lever about the hinge opposite the slit, which concentrates pressure near the slit and is not modelled here. Treat the result as a sound comparative figure rather than as a certified one, and keep more margin on a single-slit design.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my clamp need such a large safety factor?

Because the whole capacity rests on two uncertain numbers — the preload you actually achieved and the friction actually present. Neither is controlled to better than about ±25 %.

Can I add a key as well?

You can, and it is common as a safety backstop, but do not add the two capacities together. The clamp has to slip before the key takes any load at all.

Does the calculator model a double-slit (two-piece) clamp?

The contact arc reduction covers it approximately. A two-piece clamp is substantially more even in pressure than a single-slit one, so the model is conservative there and optimistic on a single slit.

Which standard is this?

There is no single standard for clamp connections. The bolt preload follows VDI 2230 practice and the friction capacity is standard machine-design work.
