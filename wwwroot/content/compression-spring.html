How a compression spring is calculated

A helical compression spring is a bar in torsion wound into a helix. Pushing on the ends twists the wire, and it is that shear stress — not bending, and not the axial force — which decides whether the spring survives. Everything on this page follows from two numbers: the spring index Dm/d, which governs stress, and the number of active coils, which governs rate.

The rate depends on the fourth power of the wire diameter and the inverse cube of the coil diameter, so small dimensional changes move it a long way. Going from 3,0 mm to 3,2 mm wire — one step in the standard range — stiffens a spring by nearly 30 %. This is why spring wire comes in a fixed series and why the calculator offers a list rather than a free field.

The equations

Rate from torsion of the wire, stress with the Wahl correction for curvature:

w = Dₘ / d                          spring index

R = G d⁴ / (8 Dₘ³ n)                 spring rate

τ  = 8 F Dₘ / (π d³)                nominal shear stress
k  = (4w − 1)/(4w − 4) + 0,615/w     Wahl correction
τₖ = k τ                              corrected, at the inner fibre

Lᶜ = L₀ / Dₘ                          slenderness, for buckling

The Wahl factor exists because the inside of the coil is tighter than the outside, so the shear stress there is higher than the plain torsion formula gives. At index 8 it adds 18 %; at index 4 it adds 40 %. Low-index springs are not the compact solution they look like.

Where the inputs come from

Wire diameter. From the standard series — the list on the page. A spring specified at 3,15 mm when the mill rolls 3,0 and 3,2 is a spring nobody will quote.

Spring index. Not entered, but keep an eye on it. Below 4 the wire is hard to coil and the Wahl factor punishes you; above 12 the spring is floppy and tangles in the bin. Four to twelve is the workable range and eight is a good starting point.

Load type, static or dynamic. This changes which allowable stress applies and whether a fatigue check runs at all. A spring that cycles more than about 10⁴ times in its life is dynamic, whatever the application looks like.

End support. The factor ν describes how the ends are held, and it decides the buckling limit. Both ends free on flat surfaces is the worst case at ν = 1,0; ends fixed and guided is far more stable.

Shot peening. Leave it on for a dynamic spring. It puts the surface into residual compression and typically doubles fatigue life at the same stress.

Worked example

3,0 mm EN 10270-1 SH wire, 24 mm mean coil diameter, 80 mm free length, 8 active coils, closed and ground ends, shot peened, static load, both ends on flat surfaces, working at 55 mm.

Spring index  24 / 3                     8,00
Total coils                             10,0
Solid length                            30,0 mm
Wire length                              754 mm     mass 41,8 g

Spring rate                             7,46 N/mm
Deflection at L₁ = 55                  25,00 mm
Working force  F₁                     186,5 N
Force at solid                         373,1 N

Stress at L₁                            500 MPa   → SF 1,53  ✓
Stress at solid length                  1000 MPa   → SF 0,77  ✗
Allowable  0,45 × Rᵐ                    765 MPa

Slenderness  L₀/Dₘ                       3,33   ⚠ buckles

The spring passes at its working point and fails twice on the checks around it. At solid length the stress reaches 1000 MPa against 765 allowable, so if anything ever compresses this spring fully it takes a permanent set and never returns to 80 mm. That is not a fatigue failure at 10⁶ cycles — it happens once, on the first over-travel, usually during assembly.

And at a slenderness of 3,33 with unguided flat ends it buckles sideways before reaching the working point. Both problems have the same family of fixes: shorter free length, larger coil diameter, or a guide rod. A compression spring result is never just the working stress — the solid-length and buckling rows are where real designs come apart.

Reading the result

Static safety at the working length wants 1,2 or better against 0,45 Rm. This is the easy check and it is rarely the one that fails.

Stress at solid length is the check that matters for anything a person can push on. If the spring cannot be compressed solid without yielding, either it must be mechanically prevented from getting there, or it needs redesigning. Design offices differ on this; the safe convention is that a spring should survive being pressed solid.

Buckling. Above a slenderness of about 2,6 with flat unguided ends, a compression spring will kick sideways. A guide rod or a bore fixes it, at the cost of friction and wear.

Natural frequency matters when the spring is excited near it — a valve spring is the classic case. Surge means individual coils resonate and hammer each other, and it destroys springs quickly.

Frequently asked questions

My spring passes at the working point but fails at solid length. Is that a problem?

It depends entirely on whether anything can compress it that far. If a stop prevents it, no. If a person or a mechanism can bottom it out, yes — the spring comes back shorter and weaker, permanently.

What is the difference between total and active coils?

The closed end coils do not deflect, so they add length and mass but no compliance. Closed and ground ends cost two coils, which is why 10 total gives 8 active here.

Why does the calculator only offer certain wire diameters?

Because those are the diameters the standard wire series is drawn to. Anything else has to be specially made.

Which standard is this?

EN 13906-1 for the design method, with wire grades from EN 10270-1, -2 and -3.
