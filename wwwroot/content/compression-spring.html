How a compression spring is calculated

A helical compression spring is a bar in torsion wound into a helix. Pushing on the ends twists the wire, and it is that shear stress — not bending, and not the axial force — which decides whether the spring survives. Everything on this page follows from two numbers: the spring index Dm/d, which governs stress, and the number of active coils, which governs rate.

The rate depends on the fourth power of the wire diameter and the inverse cube of the coil diameter, so small dimensional changes move it a long way. Going from 3,0 mm to 3,2 mm wire — one step in the standard range — stiffens a spring by nearly 30 %. This is why spring wire comes in a fixed series and why the calculator offers a list rather than a free field.

The equations

Rate from torsion of the wire, stress with the Wahl correction for curvature:

w = Dₘ / d                          spring index

R = G d⁴ / (8 Dₘ³ n)                 spring rate

τ  = 8 F Dₘ / (π d³)                nominal shear stress
k  = (4w − 1)/(4w − 4) + 0,615/w     Wahl correction
tau_k = k tau                           corrected, at the inner fibre

lambda      = L_0 / D_m                 slenderness
lambda_eff  = nu . lambda               effective slenderness

s_K = L_0 . (0,5/(1 - G/E))
          . [1 - sqrt(1 - ((1 - G/E)/(0,5 + G/E)) (pi D_m/(nu L_0))^2)]

S_a = (0,0015 D_m^2/d + 0,1 d) . n      minimum coil clearance

ν is a buckling-LENGTH factor, exactly as in Euler's column. The buckling length is ν·L₀, so a larger ν buckles more easily, and the effective slenderness is ν·λ rather than λ/ν. Ends ground and held parallel against flat plates cannot rotate, which makes them the fixed–fixed case at ν = 0,5 — the most stable of the set, not the middle of it. ν = 1 is both ends hinged, and ν = 2 is one end built in with the other entirely free.

The spring buckles once its working deflection reaches sK. When the term under the root goes negative the coil is too short and fat for the instability to exist at all, at any deflection.

The Wahl factor exists because the inside of the coil is tighter than the outside, so the shear stress there is higher than the plain torsion formula gives. At index 8 it adds 18 %; at index 4 it adds 40 %. Low-index springs are not the compact solution they look like.

Where the inputs come from

Wire diameter. From the standard series — the list on the page. A spring specified at 3,15 mm when the mill rolls 3,0 and 3,2 is a spring nobody will quote.

Spring index. Not entered, but keep an eye on it. Below 4 the wire is hard to coil and the Wahl factor punishes you; above 12 the spring is floppy and tangles in the bin. Four to twelve is the workable range and eight is a good starting point.

Load type, static or dynamic. This changes which allowable stress applies and whether a fatigue check runs at all. A spring that cycles more than about 10⁴ times in its life is dynamic, whatever the application looks like.

End support. The factor ν describes how the ends are held and it sets the buckling length. The usual case — ends ground and resting on flat faces that stay parallel — is the most stable of the set at ν = 0,5, because neither end can rotate. The worst case is one end built in with the other entirely free, ν = 2,0. A guide rod or bore prevents buckling outright and is not one of these cases at all.

Shot peening. Leave it on for a dynamic spring. It puts the surface into residual compression and typically doubles fatigue life at the same stress.

Worked example

3,0 mm EN 10270-1 SH wire, 24 mm mean coil diameter, 80 mm free length, 8 active coils, closed and ground ends, shot peened, static load, both ends on flat surfaces, working at 55 mm.

Total coils                             10,0
Solid length                            30,0 mm
Wire length                              754 mm     mass 41,8 g

Spring rate                             7,46 N/mm
Deflection at L₁ = 55                  25,00 mm
Working force  F₁                     186,5 N
Force at solid                         373,1 N

Stress at L₁                            500 MPa   → SF 1,53  ✓
Stress at solid length                  1000 MPa   → SF 0,77  ✗
Allowable  0,45 x R_m                   765 MPa

Slenderness  lambda = L_0/D_m            3,33
Minimum coil clearance  S_a              4,70 mm

Buckling, by seating:
  ends flat and parallel  nu 0,5    no buckling at any deflection
  one end fixed, one hinged  nu 0,7  no buckling at any deflection
  both ends hinged        nu 1,0    s_K 24,3 mm  -> buckles at 25 mm
  one end fixed, one free nu 2,0    s_K  5,2 mm  -> buckles

The spring passes at its working point and fails on the check beside it. At solid length the stress reaches 1000 MPa against 765 allowable, so if anything ever compresses this spring fully it takes a permanent set and never returns to 80 mm. That is not a fatigue failure at 10⁶ cycles — it happens once, on the first over-travel, usually during assembly.

Buckling depends entirely on how the ends are held, and the four lines above are the same spring at the same 25 mm of deflection. Held parallel between flat faces it cannot buckle at any deflection; with one end free it goes at about 5 mm. That is a factor of four in seating alone, and it is why ν is an input rather than a constant.

The coil clearance is the other row to read. EN 13906-1 asks for Sa = 4,70 mm still open between the coils at the largest working force; this spring leaves 25 mm, so it is comfortable. A clearance merely greater than zero is not the test — a spring that runs solid under load hammers its end coils.

Reading the result

Static safety at the working length wants 1,2 or better against 0,45 Rm. This is the easy check and it is rarely the one that fails.

That static check is a conservative simplification of EN 13906-1, not the standard's own method. It compares the corrected stress (Wahl k) against 0,45·Rm. The standard checks a statically loaded spring on the uncorrected stress against roughly 0,5·Rm, and applies the correction factor only to a dynamically loaded one — using Bergsträsser's form, k = (w + 0,5)/(w − 0,75), rather than Wahl's. On the worked example that is a safety factor of 1,53 here against about 2,01 the standard's way, so roughly 1,3 times conservative. Both correction factors are shown in the results so a figure can be traced to the one it came from. Rm is also taken as a single value per wire grade, where EN 10270 makes it fall with increasing wire diameter — a thick wire is therefore credited with more strength than the standard allows, and a thin one with less.

Stress at solid length is the check that matters for anything a person can push on. If the spring cannot be compressed solid without yielding, either it must be mechanically prevented from getting there, or it needs redesigning. Design offices differ on this; the safe convention is that a spring should survive being pressed solid.

Buckling. Read sK, not the slenderness alone: the results give the deflection at which this spring goes unstable for the seating you chose, and compare it with the deflection you asked for. Get the seating right before anything else — it moves the answer by a factor of four on the worked example, and a larger ν is the less stable case. A guide rod or a bore removes the problem entirely, at the cost of friction and wear.

Coil clearance. EN 13906-1 wants at least Sa = (0,0015·Dm²/d + 0,1·d)·n left between the coils at the largest working force, so the spring never reaches solid in service. Clearance greater than zero is not enough on its own.

Natural frequency matters when the spring is excited near it — a valve spring is the classic case. Surge means individual coils resonate and hammer each other, and it destroys springs quickly.

Frequently asked questions

My spring passes at the working point but fails at solid length. Is that a problem?

It depends entirely on whether anything can compress it that far. If a stop prevents it, no. If a person or a mechanism can bottom it out, yes — the spring comes back shorter and weaker, permanently.

What is the difference between total and active coils?

The closed end coils do not deflect, so they add length and mass but no compliance. Closed and ground ends cost two coils, which is why 10 total gives 8 active here.

Why does the calculator only offer certain wire diameters?

Because those are the diameters the standard wire series is drawn to. Anything else has to be specially made.

Which standard is this?

EN 13906-1 for the design method, with wire grades from EN 10270-1, -2 and -3.
