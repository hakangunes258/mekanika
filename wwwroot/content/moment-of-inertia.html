How section properties are calculated

The second moment of area — commonly called the moment of inertia — describes how a cross-section's material is distributed relative to a bending axis. Material far from the axis contributes far more than material near it, because the contribution goes as the square of the distance. This single fact explains why an I-beam looks the way it does and why a tube is so much better than a solid bar of the same weight.

Two quantities come out of it and they answer different questions. I governs deflection — how far the beam bends under load. W = I/c governs stress — whether it breaks. A section can be excellent at one and mediocre at the other, so a beam chosen on stiffness alone may still be overstressed, and vice versa.

The polar moment Ip is the correct torsional constant only for a circular section. For an open section such as a channel or an angle the torsion constant It is a different and much smaller quantity — one to two orders of magnitude — and the calculator reports it separately.

The product of inertia Ixy is what makes an angle different. When it is not zero, bending about x alone still deflects the section sideways, and the axes the section is actually weak about are rotated. The calculator gives Iu, Iv, the angle and rv whenever that happens.

Where the inputs come from

The shape. Pick the one that matches your section. The dimension fields change with it, and every shape is computed from its own closed-form expressions rather than by numerical approximation.

Which axis is which. x is the horizontal axis and bending about it uses the height; y is vertical and uses the width. Getting these the wrong way round is the most common error on this page, and on a 100 × 150 rectangle it is a factor of 2,25 in the answer.

Material and profile length. These affect only the mass figures, never I or W — section properties are pure geometry. They are there so you can weigh a design without leaving the page.

Symmetry. For a symmetric section the distances to the top and bottom fibres are equal and there is one section modulus per axis. For an asymmetric one they differ, the smaller c gives the larger W, and the stress must be checked on the side with the larger c.

Worked example

A solid rectangular bar, 100 mm wide and 150 mm tall, in C45, one metre long.

Area                              15 000,00 mm²
Mass per metre                      117,750 kg/m

Iₓ   (bending about x, uses h)     2,812 × 10⁷ mm⁴
Iₖ   (bending about y, uses b)     1,250 × 10⁷ mm⁴
Iᵛ                                 4,062 × 10⁷ mm⁴

Wₓ                                375 000,00 mm³
Wₖ                                250 000,00 mm³

rₓ                                    43,30 mm
rₖ                                    28,87 mm

c to top / bottom                      75,00 mm
c to left / right                      50,00 mm

Lay the same bar on its side and I falls from 2,812 × 10⁷ to 1,250 × 10⁷ mm⁴ — the beam becomes 2,25 times more flexible without a gram of material changing. The ratio is (150/100)², because I scales with the cube of the depth and the section only got 1,5 times shallower.

Now hollow it out. The same 150 × 100 outline with an 8 mm wall (inner 134 × 84) drops to 29,390 kg/m — a quarter of the mass — and keeps Ix = 1,128 × 10⁷ mm⁴, which is 40 % of the solid bar's. Per kilogram that is 1,6 times the bending stiffness, because the material removed was all close to the neutral axis where y² is small and it was contributing almost nothing. That is the entire argument for hollow and I-shaped sections, and it is visible in the numbers rather than a matter of opinion.

Reading the result

Use I for deflection and W for stress. If your beam is too bendy, you need more I — get the material further from the axis. If it is overstressed, you need more W, which usually means the same thing but not always.

Check both axes. A section strong about x may be weak about y, and a beam loaded slightly off-plane, or one free to buckle sideways, will find that weakness.

The radius of gyration is for buckling, and it is the principal one. A column's slenderness is its effective length divided by the smallest radius of gyration the section has. For a rectangle, an I-beam, a channel or a T that is the smaller of rx and ry — 28,87 mm on the 100×150 rectangle, not 43,30. For an angle it is neither. An equal-leg L 100×100×12 has rx = ry = 30,51 mm and rv = 19,57 mm: its weak axis runs at 45° through the heel, which is why an angle strut buckles diagonally. Using 30,51 understates the slenderness by a factor of 1,56 and overstates the Euler load by 2,4. The calculator prints ru and rv whenever Ixy ≠ 0 and says which to use.

The polar moment is not a torsion constant. Ip = Ix + Iy is what you want for a round bar or a tube, and for nothing else. The calculator now gives It separately — the St Venant series for a solid rectangle, Bredt for a closed box, Σb·t³/3 for an open section — and the gap is not small: on the I 100×200 above Ip is 115 times It. Warping restraint is not included, and on an open section it usually carries most of the torque in practice.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as mass moment of inertia?

No, and the shared name causes constant confusion. This page gives the second moment of area in mm⁴, used for bending and buckling. Mass moment of inertia is in kg·m² and describes resistance to angular acceleration.

Why does the material make no difference to I?

Because I is purely geometric. Material enters through E when you calculate deflection, and through the allowable stress when you calculate strength — an aluminium and a steel beam of the same shape have identical I and very different stiffness.

Which section modulus do I use on an asymmetric section?

The smaller one. It corresponds to the fibre furthest from the centroid, which is where the stress is highest.

How do I combine two shapes?

Compute each about its own centroid, then shift each to the common centroid with the parallel axis theorem, I' = I + Ae², and add. This page handles one shape at a time.
