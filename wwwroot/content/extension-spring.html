How an extension spring is calculated

An extension spring is a compression spring turned inside out, with two differences that dominate the design. It is wound with the coils touching, so it carries an initial tension that must be exceeded before it extends at all. And it has to be attached to something, which means hooks — and the hook, not the body, is where extension springs break.

The body calculation is the familiar torsion-of-the-wire problem. The hook is a different load case altogether: the wire there is bent, not twisted, and it is bent around a small radius that concentrates the stress. A spring body with a comfortable safety factor and a hook running at its limit is the normal result, and reading only the body number is the usual way to design a spring that fails.

The equations

Body rate and stress as for a compression spring, plus a separate hook check:

R = G d⁴ / (8 Dₘ³ n)                  spring rate
F = F₀ + R s                          force, including initial tension

τₖ = k · 8 F Dₘ / (π d³)              body shear stress, Wahl corrected

sigma_A = K_1 . 32 F a / (pi d^3) + 4F/(pi d^2)   hook bending, at the body
tau_H   = K_2 . 8 F D_m / (pi d^3)               hook shear, at the bend

K_1 = (4C_1^2 - C_1 - 1)/(4C_1(C_1 - 1))    C_1 = D_m/d   loop radius
K_2 = (4C_2 - 1)/(4C_2 - 4)                 C_2 = 2R_1/d  bend radius

The bending moment arm is the radius of the LOOP, not the bend radius. The load comes in through the middle of the loop, so the section where the hook meets the body carries M = F·Dm/2 — 8 mm on this spring, against the 3,5 mm bend radius. Using the bend radius halves the bending stress and makes the model run backwards as well: a larger bend would come out as more stress, where in reality a generous bend relieves the concentration.

The two concentrations are separate and neither can fall below 1. Bending is concentrated by the loop curvature, torsion by the bend itself, and a bend always concentrates — a factor under 1 would make the hook less stressed than the plain body, which no geometry can do. The hook type acts on the moment arm, which is where a raised hook earns its lower stress: it brings the load line closer to the body axis.

Where the inputs come from

Initial tension. This is a manufacturing property, not a free choice. The calculator shows a recommended band for the wire and index you have entered; asking a spring maker for tension outside it means asking for a spring they cannot reliably wind. Cold-wound springs have it; hot-wound ones do not.

Hook type. The most consequential dropdown on the page. A German hook (full loop) is cheap and universal and is also the most highly stressed. A raised hook lowers the bending stress by shortening the moment arm. A threaded insert removes the hook problem entirely and costs more.

The two operating positions. Enter both, not just the working one. The difference between them is the stress range, and that is what the fatigue check uses. An extension spring cycling between two loaded positions is far better off than one that returns to zero each time.

Active coils. Long extension springs are normal — 40 coils here — because rate falls with n and extension springs usually want to be soft over a long stroke.

Worked example

2,0 mm EN 10270-1 SH wire, 16 mm mean coil diameter, 40 active coils, German hooks, 20 N of initial tension, working between 10 mm and 30 mm of extension.

Fatigue   body (corrected stress)        SF 2,98
          hook                           SF 2,27   governing

Read the force at position 1: 29,9 N, not 9,95 N. The initial tension of 20 N is carried before the spring moves at all, so an extension spring does not pass through the origin and a rate calculation alone understates the force by that whole amount. On a light spring like this one the initial tension is two-thirds of the working force.

The hook governs everything here — 2,07 in bending against 2,55 for the body, and the same again on fatigue. That is the normal result for an extension spring and it is why the maximum force is 103 N rather than the 150 N the body alone would take: quoting the body's limit as the maximum extension gives a number the hook cannot survive.

Move to a raised hook and the moment arm shortens, taking the governing factor back towards the body — which is where you want it. A threaded insert removes the hook stresses altogether, at the cost of a thread to check instead.

Reading the result

Compare the body and hook factors and design to the lower one. If the hook governs, change the hook, not the wire — thicker wire raises the hook stress too, because the bend radius scales with it.

Check the initial tension against the recommended band. Below it the coils may not close reliably; above it the spring is hard to wind and the tension will not be repeatable from batch to batch.

Fatigue is driven by the stress range between your two positions, and it is checked at both the body and the hook. The body runs on the corrected stress, as EN 13906-2 requires for a dynamically loaded spring; the hook is included because that is where extension springs actually break, and on the worked example it is the governing one. The cycle estimate beside it is a simplified S–N curve for the body alone — do not read an "infinite life" there as covering the hook.

There is no buckling check, because a spring in tension cannot buckle. There is also no solid-length equivalent: the limit is the maximum extension shown in the results, and that figure is now the lower of what the body and the hook allow. Taking the body's limit alone overstated it by half on the worked example — 150 N against the 103 N the hook can carry.

Frequently asked questions

Why is there force at zero deflection?

Initial tension. The spring is wound with its coils pressed together, and that preload has to be overcome before it starts to extend. It is a real, useful property — it is what keeps a slack spring from rattling.

Why does the hook fail before the body?

Because the wire at the hook is bent around a small radius rather than twisted along its length, and the sharp bend concentrates stress. It is the standard failure location for extension springs.

Can I get an extension spring without initial tension?

Yes, but it must be wound with a gap between coils, which costs stroke and is more expensive. Hot-wound springs have no initial tension at all.

Which standard is this?

EN 13906-2 for the design method, with wire grades from EN 10270.
