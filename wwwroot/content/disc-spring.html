How a disc spring is calculated

A disc spring is a shallow truncated cone. Pressing it flat does not bend it the way a beam bends: the cross-section stays very nearly rigid and rotates about a neutral point, which drags each material point radially inwards or outwards depending on whether it sits above or below that point. The circumference it occupies changes, and the stress that follows is a tangential one. That single picture — the Almen–Laszlo model DIN 2092 is built on — explains everything else on this page.

Two consequences are worth having in mind before you read a result. First, the stress at a point varies as −z/r: its sign depends only on which face the point is on, and its size falls off with radius. So the two upper-face corners are in compression and the two lower-face corners in tension, and the outer-edge pair is smaller than the inner-edge pair by exactly the diameter ratio. Second, the force is a cubic in the deflection, not a straight line. A disc spring is chosen precisely because its rate falls as it is compressed, and with a tall enough cone the rate reaches zero before the disc is flat.

The equations

Everything comes from four shape factors and the two elastic constants:

delta = De/Di                              diameter ratio
h0    = l0 - t'                            cone height = travel to flat

K1 = (1/pi) . ((delta-1)/delta)^2 / ( (delta+1)/(delta-1) - 2/ln(delta) )
K2 = (6/pi) . ( (delta-1)/ln(delta) - 1 ) / ln(delta)
K3 = (3/pi) . (delta-1)/ln(delta)
K4 = 1                                     no contact surfaces

F = 4E/(1-nu^2) . t'^4/(K1 De^2) . K4^2 . (s/t')
    . [ K4^2 (h0/t' - s/t')(h0/t' - s/(2t')) + 1 ]          force, one disc

dF/ds = 4E/(1-nu^2) . t'^3/(K1 De^2) . K4^2
        . [ K4^2 ( (h0/t')^2 - 3(h0/t')(s/t') + 1.5(s/t')^2 ) + 1 ]

W = 2E/(1-nu^2) . t'^5/(K1 De^2) . K4^2 . (s/t')^2
    . [ K4^2 (h0/t' - s/(2t'))^2 + 1 ]                      energy, one disc

The four corner stresses share one common factor c = 4E/(1−ν²) · t' s/(K1De²), and differ only in a sign and in the 1/δ:

X = h0/t' - s/(2t')

sigma_I   = -c . K4 . [ K4 . K2 . X + K3 ]              upper face, inner edge
sigma_II  = -c . K4 . [ K4 . K2 . X - K3 ]              lower face, inner edge
sigma_III = +c . K4 . (1/delta) . [ K4 (K2 - 2 K3) X + K3 ]   lower face, outer edge
sigma_IV  = +c . K4 . (1/delta) . [ K4 (K2 - 2 K3) X - K3 ]   upper face, outer edge

Those four are not four independent formulas. Subtracting the pairs leaves the identity

sigma_III - sigma_IV = (sigma_II - sigma_I) / delta

at every deflection, which is the −z/r field written out: the part of the stress that flips sign between the two faces is the same at both radii apart from the 1/r. It is also the cheapest check that all four have been transcribed correctly, and the calculator's test suite asserts it rather than asserting four numbers.

Springs with contact surfaces — the DIN 2093 Group 3 construction, for thicknesses above 6 mm — replace t by the reduced thickness t' and pick up K4 from DIN 2092's C1/C2 expression. Setting t' = t collapses that expression to K4 = 1 exactly, for any l0/t, which is why there is one set of equations here and not two.

Where the inputs come from

l0 is the whole free height, not the cone height. This is the one input people get wrong. A catalogue gives the overall height of the free disc with its thickness included; the travel available is h0 = l0 − t', which is a much smaller number. On the worked example below l0 is 3.15 mm and the whole travel is 0.90 mm.

The shape number h0/t' is the design decision. It, and not the diameter, is what makes a disc spring stiff or soft and what decides how strongly the rate falls. The three DIN 2093 series are three choices of it: about 0.40 for series A (stiff, short travel), 0.75 for B, and 1.30 for C (soft, long travel, close to the stability limit). The calculator reports which one your dimensions resemble, and says "between series" rather than guessing when they sit in the middle.

δ wants to be near 2. DIN 2093 sizes sit between about 2.0 and 2.3. The Almen–Laszlo model loses accuracy as δ approaches 1 — the factors all carry 1/ln δ, which blows up there — and a very wide ring is poorly described by a rigid rotating section. The calculator warns outside 1.5–3.5.

E and ν matter more than the grade name. Both are editable for that reason: every force and every stress carries 1/(1 − ν²), and the force is linear in E. DIN 2093 is written for 51CrV4 and C60S at E = 206 000 MPa and ν = 0.30; a stainless disc of the same dimensions at 185 000 MPa is 10 % softer on E alone, and about 8 % once its higher ν is counted the other way.

Contact surfaces are a construction, not a finish. Grinding a flat on the loaded edges shortens the lever arm, which stiffens the disc (K4 rises above 1); DIN 2093 then takes thickness back out so the catalogue force stays comparable. The two pull opposite ways, so neither can be left out of a calculation. Below 6 mm thickness the question does not arise.

n and i are two different multipliers. Discs nested the same way round — in parallel — multiply force and leave travel alone. Sets stacked alternately, face to face, multiply travel and leave force alone. Both change the free length of the column, and only the parallel count adds thickness to it.

The permissible upper stress is optional and has to come from the standard. DIN 2093's fatigue diagrams are charts of permissible upper stress against lower stress, one per thickness group and per life. They are not reproduced here, because reading one off from memory would put an invented number straight into a safety factor. The calculator gives you the two stresses to enter the chart with, and then checks against whatever you read off it.

Read the two static lines together. At the deflection asked for the disc is at 2086 MPa, a factor of 1.25 on the 2600 MPa practice figure — fine. Taken all the way to flat the same disc would reach 2680 MPa, a factor of 0.97. That is not a badly chosen example: a well-formed series A shape is always past the figure at flat, and it is most of the reason DIN 2092 puts the recommended travel limit at 0.75 h0. The travel has to be limited by a stop, not by an instruction — and the two rows are reported separately for exactly that reason, because one number cannot say both things and a red verdict on the flat figure would condemn a perfectly usable design.

Now look at what the last quarter of the travel buys. Going from 0.675 mm to flat adds 0.225 mm of travel and 1955 N of force per disc, while the stress at point I rises by 594 MPa. The force per millimetre is falling and the stress per millimetre is not — that is the whole trade a disc spring makes.

And what backing off buys, which is less than you would think. At half the travel the stress at point I is 1441 MPa: 54 % of the flat value, not 50 %. The stress carries a factor s but multiplies a bracket that grows as the deflection falls, so the curve is concave. The first part of the travel costs more stress per millimetre than the last.

The stack figures are two separate multiplications. Two in parallel doubled the force to 13 000 N without changing the travel of a disc; three in series tripled the travel to 2.025 mm without changing the force. The free length gained the material thickness once, for the second disc of each parallel pair: 3 × (3.15 + 2.25) = 16.2 mm, not 3 × 3.15.

Reading the result

Point I for static strength, points II and III for fatigue. Point I is the largest of the four in magnitude and it is compressive, which is why it is compared against a practice figure rather than against the yield strength: it is a calculated edge stress that a real disc relieves by yielding locally on its first few strokes. The tensile points are the ones a crack starts from, so those are what DIN 2093 assesses fatigue on — whichever of the two is larger. Which one that is depends on the shape number, so the calculator picks it at the upper position and then reads the lower stress at the same point; a range taken between two different points is not a range.

Check the shape number against the snap-through limit. The tangent rate reaches zero at the flat position when K4 h0/t' = √2 ≈ 1.414, and past that the force falls over the last part of the travel: the disc becomes unstable and can snap through. This is exact, not a rule of thumb, and it is a property of the shape alone — no load, material or diameter enters it. Series C sits at 1.30, deliberately just inside.

A high shape number is also how you get a near-constant force. A 1 mm thick disc at De 40 with l0 2.30 has h0/t = 1.30 and carries 1016 N at 0.75 h0 against 1072 N at flat — 5 % more force over the last quarter of its travel. That flat region is why disc springs are used to hold a preload while something wears or expands, and it is the one thing a helical spring cannot do.

The tangent rate and the secant rate are different numbers. Both are reported. The tangent is dF/ds at one deflection and is what you want for stability and resonance; the secant is the average over the stroke and is what you want for "what does this travel cost me in force". On the worked example above a single disc has a tangent rate of 8784 N/mm at position 2 and a secant rate of 9254 N/mm over the stroke.

Treating a Group 3 disc as if it had no contact surfaces is not conservative. A 140 × 72 × 8, l0 10.5 disc calculated with its contact surfaces reaches 100.2 kN at flat; calculated as a plain disc of the same dimensions it reads 86.6 kN. That is 14 % under the real force, so the error is in the direction that matters. Both halves of the construction are doing work: t' = 7.52 mm takes force out through t'⁴, K4 = 1.081 puts it back, and h0 = l0 − t' is 2.98 mm rather than 2.50.

What is not in any of these numbers. Friction — between discs nested in parallel and against the guide — makes the loading branch stiffer than the figures and the unloading branch softer, by a few percent per interface. Setting: a disc spring loses force over its life, most of it on the first strokes and most of it when worked near flat. Temperature: E falls and relaxation rises, and the calculation runs at whatever E you entered. None of the three is modelled, and all three push the same way on a stack that is worked hard.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the permissible stress higher than the yield strength?

Because σI is a theoretical stress at a sharp edge, computed from a model that treats the section as rigid. The real disc yields locally there on its first strokes, redistributes the peak and then behaves elastically. The 2600 MPa figure for spring steel is calibrated to that behaviour, not to first yield.

Parallel or series?

Parallel for force, series for travel. Two in parallel double the force at the same travel; two in series double the travel at the same force. Mixing them gives any combination — that is the main reason disc springs are specified at all.

Why does the rate fall as the spring is compressed?

Because flattening the cone reduces the lever arm the load acts through. It is geometry, not material, which is why the effect scales with the shape number h0/t' and vanishes for a nearly flat washer.

Can I work the spring all the way to flat?

Mechanically yes, and catalogues quote the force at flat. But DIN 2092 recommends 0.75 h0 as the travel limit: the model is least reliable in the last quarter, the stresses are highest there, and the setting loss over life is largest there. Design a hard stop.

Why is there no fatigue life in cycles?

Because the permissible stress comes from DIN 2093's own diagrams, which are not reproduced here. The calculator gives the upper and lower stress at the governing tensile point; read the limit off the diagram for your thickness group and enter it, and the comparison is then made on the page.

Which standard is this?

DIN 2092 for the calculation, with the thickness groups and the series ratios of DIN 2093. The dimension tables of DIN 2093 are not embedded — dimensions are entered freely.
