How a bolted joint is calculated

A bolted joint is not a bolt carrying a load. It is a spring in tension — the bolt — clamped against a much stiffer spring in compression — the parts. When an external force pulls the joint apart, only a small fraction of it reaches the bolt; the rest simply unloads the parts. That fraction is the load factor Φ, and on a typical steel joint it is under 0,2. This is why a correctly preloaded bolt barely feels a fluctuating load, and why an under-preloaded one fails in fatigue within weeks.

The calculation therefore has almost nothing to do with the external force and almost everything to do with preload. VDI 2230 works through it in a fixed order: find how much clamp force the joint must retain, add what will be lost to embedding, add the part of the external load that unloads the parts, and only then check that a real bolt tightened by a real method can deliver that much without yielding.

The equations

The two resiliences set the load factor, and the load factor governs everything after it:

δₛ = Σ lᵢ / (Eₛ Aᵢ)          bolt resilience
δᵣ = lᴸ / (Eᵣ Aᵣₑₗ)          part resilience, deformation cone

Φₙ = n · δᵣ / (δₛ + δᵣ)      load factor with load introduction

Fₘᵣᵣₙ = Fᵏₕ₀ + Fᵦ + (1 − Φₙ) Fₐₘₐₓ    minimum preload needed
Fₘᵣᵣₐₛ = αₐ · Fₘᵣᵣₙ                        maximum, after tightening scatter

αₐ is the tightening factor: 1,6 for a torque wrench, 1,2 for angle control, 1,0 for a yield-controlled tool. It multiplies the preload you must be able to survive, so the tightening method changes the bolt size.

Where the inputs come from

Required clamp force. This is a design decision, not a lookup. It is whatever the joint must still have left after everything else has been subtracted — enough to prevent slip, prevent gapping, or seal a face. If you leave it at zero you are telling the calculator the joint may go loose.

External load, minimum and maximum. Enter the range, not just the peak. The amplitude between them is what drives fatigue, and a joint can be perfectly safe statically while failing at 10⁶ cycles.

Friction coefficients. Three separate ones: thread, head/nut, and the interface between the parts. 0,12 is a reasonable dry-and-lightly-oiled default. These change the achieved preload more than anything else on the page.

Tightening method. Choose honestly. A torque wrench carries αₐ = 1,6, meaning the joint must tolerate 60 % more preload than the minimum — that scatter is often what forces the next bolt size up.

Bolting type SV1 to SV6. This describes where the load enters the joint relative to the bolt head. It sets the load introduction factor n, and moving the load introduction deeper into the joint sharply reduces what the bolt feels.

Worked example

An M12 class 8.8 through-bolt with nut and washers, clamping 30 mm of S235JR, substitute diameter 36 mm, all friction at 0,12, tightened with a torque wrench to 90 % of yield, load applied at the head (SV1), external load swinging between 2 kN and 10 kN, and the joint must retain 5 kN of clamp force.

Load factor Φₙ                     0,0474
Embedding                          23,5 µm   → preload loss 7,60 kN
Part of Fₐ reaching the bolt       0,47 kN   of 10 kN applied

Minimum required preload          22,12 kN   → Mₐ 41,7 Nm
Maximum required preload          35,39 kN   → Mₐ 66,7 Nm
Bolt capacity at 90 % yield       37,77 kN   → Mₐ 71,2 Nm

Yielding (assembly)                 1,19    ⚠ low
Surface pressure under head         1,91    ✓
Fatigue                            22,21    ✓  infinite life

The striking number is 0,47 kN. Ten kilonewtons are applied to the joint and less than half a kilonewton reaches the bolt — the other 9,5 kN simply relieves the compressed plates. That is the entire reason preloaded joints survive fatigue, and it is why the fatigue safety factor comes out at 22 rather than near 1.

The tight number is assembly yielding at 1,19. The gap between 22,12 kN needed and 35,39 kN worst case is nothing but torque-wrench scatter. Switch the tightening method to angle control and αₐ drops from 1,6 to about 1,2, the maximum preload falls with it, and the same bolt stops being marginal — without changing a single part.

Reading the result

Yielding at assembly compares the worst-case preload against the bolt's capacity at your chosen utilisation. Below 1,0 the bolt yields while being tightened. Raising the utilisation limit toward 100 % is legitimate and is what yield-controlled tightening exists for.

Surface pressure under the head and nut is the check people forget. Exceed the permissible pressure and the bolt does not break — it sinks into the part, the preload disappears, and the joint fails later for no visible reason. Washers are the usual fix.

Fatigue uses the stress amplitude, which depends on Φₙ and therefore on joint stiffness, not on how strong the bolt is. A higher class of bolt does almost nothing for fatigue; a longer, more resilient bolt does a great deal.

Loss of clamp reads 1,00 whenever you enter a required clamp force. That is by construction, not a result: the minimum preload is sized to deliver exactly the clamp force you asked for, so the ratio is always one. Read it as "the preload was sized to meet your requirement", and put your margin into the required clamp force itself.

Frequently asked questions

Why does so little of the external load reach the bolt?

Because the clamped parts are far stiffer than the bolt — here about six times. The load divides in proportion to resilience, so the bolt takes the smaller share. This is the central idea of VDI 2230.

Should I use a longer bolt or a stronger one?

For fatigue, longer. Length increases bolt resilience, which lowers Φ and therefore the stress amplitude. For static strength, stronger.

What is embedding and why does it cost me preload?

The microscopic peaks on every clamped surface flatten under load. Here that is 23,5 µm across the joint, which on this bolt is 7,6 kN of lost preload — more than the external load. It has to be added to the preload, which is exactly what the calculation does.

Which standard is this?

VDI 2230 Part 1, using ISO 898-1 property classes and ISO metric thread geometry.
