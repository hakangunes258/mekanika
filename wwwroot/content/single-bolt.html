How a bolted joint is calculated

A bolted joint is not a bolt carrying a load. It is a spring in tension — the bolt — clamped against a much stiffer spring in compression — the parts. When an external force pulls the joint apart, only a small fraction of it reaches the bolt; the rest simply unloads the parts. That fraction is the load factor Φ, and on a typical steel joint it is under 0,2. This is why a correctly preloaded bolt barely feels a fluctuating load, and why an under-preloaded one fails in fatigue within weeks.

The calculation therefore has almost nothing to do with the external force and almost everything to do with preload. VDI 2230 works through it in a fixed order: find how much clamp force the joint must retain, add what will be lost to embedding, add the part of the external load that unloads the parts, and only then check that a real bolt tightened by a real method can deliver that much without yielding.

The equations

The two resiliences set the load factor, and the load factor governs everything after it:

delta_S = sum(l_i) / (E_S A_i)          bolt resilience
delta_P = l_K / (E_P A_ers)             part resilience, deformation cone

Phi_n   = n . delta_P / (delta_S + delta_P)   load factor with load introduction

F_Mmin  = F_Kerf + F_Z + (1 - Phi_n) F_Amax   minimum preload needed
F_Mmax  = alpha_A . F_Mmin                    maximum, after tightening scatter

F_KQerf = F_Q / (q_F mu_T)              clamp force against slipping
F_KMerf = M_B . A / W                   clamp force against gapping

sigma_red = sqrt(sigma_z^2 + 3 tau^2)   assembly equivalent stress

αA is the tightening factor: 1,6 for a torque wrench, 1,0 for an angle- or yield-controlled tool, and 2,5 to 4 for an unadjusted impact wrench. It multiplies the preload the bolt must survive, so the tightening method changes the bolt size. The two methods at 1,0 reach that figure by taking the bolt to its yield point, which is why the results report no yield safety factor for them.

The last three lines are what a transverse force, a working moment and the thread's friction torque each add. All three used to be missing from the calculation as well as from this list.

Where the inputs come from

Required clamp force. This is a design decision, not a lookup. It is whatever the joint must still have left after everything else has been subtracted — enough to prevent slip, prevent gapping, or seal a face. If you leave it at zero you are telling the calculator the joint may go loose.

External load, minimum and maximum. Enter the range, not just the peak. The amplitude between them is what drives fatigue, and a joint can be perfectly safe statically while failing at 10⁶ cycles.

Friction coefficients. Three separate ones: thread, head/nut, and the interface between the parts. 0,12 is a reasonable dry-and-lightly-oiled default. These change the achieved preload more than anything else on the page.

Tightening method. Choose honestly. A torque wrench carries αₐ = 1,6, meaning the joint must tolerate 60 % more preload than the minimum — that scatter is often what forces the next bolt size up.

Bolting type. The list offers SV1 to SV3 for a through-bolt and SV4 to SV6 for a tapped hole, so only three are selectable at a time. This describes where the load enters the joint relative to the bolt head. It sets the load introduction factor n, and moving the load introduction deeper into the joint sharply reduces what the bolt feels.

Worked example

An M12 class 8.8 through-bolt with nut and washers, clamping 30 mm of S235JR, substitute diameter 36 mm, all friction at 0,12, tightened with a torque wrench to 90 % of yield, load applied at the head (SV1), external load swinging between 2 kN and 10 kN, and the joint must retain 5 kN of clamp force.

The striking number is 1,25 kN. Ten kilonewtons are applied to the joint and barely a tenth of it reaches the bolt — the other 8,75 kN simply relieves the compressed plates. That is the entire reason preloaded joints survive fatigue, and it is why the fatigue safety factor comes out at 8,4 rather than near 1.

The tight numbers are assembly yielding at 1,18 and the clamp force margin at 1,28. The gap between 22,22 kN needed and 35,56 kN worst case is nothing but torque-wrench scatter: αA = 1,6. Switch the tightening method to angle or yield control and αA falls to about 1,0, the maximum preload falls with it, and the same bolt stops being marginal — without changing a single part. Note what the results then say about yielding: those methods take the bolt to its yield point on purpose, so the row reads "n/a" rather than a safety factor.

Washers move the surface pressure check. The head bears on the washer at 419,6 MPa, but the part sees only 111,2 MPa, because the washer spreads the load over its own outside diameter. Comparing the 419,6 against the part's 490 MPa gave a safety factor of 1,24 for a part that is really at 4,41 — the wrong pressure against the wrong limit.

Add a transverse force and the answer changes completely. Put FQ = 4 kN on this joint and the clamp force needed for slip resistance is FQ/(qF·µT) = 33,3 kN, against the 6,4 kN this bolt can deliver. The margin collapses to 0,21 and the M12 fails on yielding, clamp force and surface pressure together. A transverse load is not a small correction to an axial one: friction is a poor way to carry shear, and that is why dowels and fitted bolts exist.

Reading the result

Yielding at assembly compares the worst-case preload against the bolt's capacity at your chosen utilisation. Below 1,0 the bolt yields while being tightened. Raising the utilisation limit toward 100 % is legitimate and is what yield-controlled tightening exists for.

Surface pressure under the head and nut is the check people forget. Exceed the permissible pressure and the bolt does not break — it sinks into the part, the preload disappears, and the joint fails later for no visible reason. Washers are the usual fix.

Fatigue uses the stress amplitude, which depends on Φₙ and therefore on joint stiffness, not on how strong the bolt is. A higher class of bolt does almost nothing for fatigue; a longer, more resilient bolt does a great deal.

The clamp force margin compares the residual clamp force the bolt can actually deliver — FM,zul/αA less the embedding loss and the part relief — against what the joint requires. It used to be FK,min/FKR,min, which is 1,00 by construction because the minimum preload is sized to deliver exactly the clamp force asked for; every reader who entered a required clamp force therefore saw a red FAIL at 1,00. The figure now moves, and below 1 it means the bolt genuinely cannot hold the joint together.

Transverse force and bending moment both reach the calculation now. They did not: the load case defaulted to axial-only and the two fields were zeroed on every run, so a reader who entered them saw identical results and had no way to tell. FQ sets the clamp force needed against slipping, and MB sets the clamp force needed to keep the interface in compression. The required clamp force is broken down by cause in the results so you can see which one governs.

What is still not modelled. Eccentric loading — VDI 2230's R3 to R5, where the load line sits at a distance a from the bolt axis — is not implemented. The field on the input form is lA, the distance to the point where the load enters the clamped parts, which Table 5.2/1 uses to find n; it is not the eccentricity, and it was labelled as such. Thread stripping in a tapped hole is checked against a practice minimum engagement by material rather than VDI's R9 calculation, and the results say so. The bending moment is treated as a concentric circular interface, which a multi-bolt flange is not.

Frequently asked questions

Why does so little of the external load reach the bolt?

Because the clamped parts are far stiffer than the bolt — here about six times. The load divides in proportion to resilience, so the bolt takes the smaller share. This is the central idea of VDI 2230.

Should I use a longer bolt or a stronger one?

For fatigue, longer. Length increases bolt resilience, which lowers Φ and therefore the stress amplitude. For static strength, stronger.

What is embedding and why does it cost me preload?

The microscopic peaks on every clamped surface flatten under load. Here that is 23,5 µm across the joint, which on this bolt is 7,6 kN of lost preload — more than the external load. It has to be added to the preload, which is exactly what the calculation does.

Which standard is this?

VDI 2230 Part 1, using ISO 898-1 property classes and ISO metric thread geometry.
