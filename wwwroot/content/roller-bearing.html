How roller bearing life is calculated

A cylindrical roller bearing carries load on a line rather than on a point. That single geometric difference runs through the whole calculation: the contact stress is lower for the same load, the dynamic rating is higher for the same envelope, and the fatigue exponent changes from 3 to 10/3. A roller bearing of the same bore and outside diameter as a ball bearing will typically carry roughly twice the radial load for the same life.

The price is that it carries essentially no thrust. An NU or N type has no flange on one ring, so axial load has nothing to react against — the shaft is free to float, which is often exactly what you want at the non-locating end of a shaft, but it means the axial column on this page stays at zero and any thrust in your machine must be taken somewhere else.

The equations

Same ISO 281 structure as a ball bearing, with the line-contact exponent:

P = fᶠ · Fₕ                        equivalent load, radial only

L₁₀  = (C / P)ᵧ                    p = 10/3 for roller bearings
L₁₀ₕ = 10⁶ L₁₀ / (60 n)

S₀ = C₀ / Fₕ

The exponent 10/3 rather than 3 makes life a little more sensitive to load, not less. Halving the load multiplies a ball bearing's life by 8 and a roller bearing's by about 10.

Where the inputs come from

The bearing, and the E suffix. Pick from the list to fill C and C₀. Note how much the E designs differ: an NU205 is rated 17,7 kN and an NU205E 29,3 kN in the same 25 × 52 × 15 envelope, because the E series uses larger rollers. That is a 60 % rating increase for the same space and it changes the life by a factor of about five.

Radial load only. If your shaft has thrust, this is not the bearing that takes it. Design the axial location separately — typically a deep groove or angular contact bearing at the other end.

Machine factor. Same meaning as on the ball bearing page, and the same warning: it multiplies P, and P is raised to 10/3.

Speed. Used only to convert revolutions into hours, but check it against the limiting speed shown in the results — a life figure computed above the bearing's speed limit is meaningless.

Worked example

An NU205E cylindrical roller bearing (25 × 52 × 15, C = 29,3 kN, C₀ = 27,7 kN) carrying 4000 N radial at 1500 rpm, with a light-shock machine factor of 1,2.

Equivalent load  P = 1,2 · 4000        4800 N
Load ratio  C / P                       6,10
Life exponent  p                        10/3

Basic rating life  L₁₀               415,68 × 10⁶ rev
                   L₁₀ₕ                 4619 hours

Static equivalent load  P₀            4,00 kN
Static safety  S₀                      6,92   ✓

Compare this against the ball bearing page, which runs a 6205 — the same 25 × 52 × 15 envelope. The 6205 manages 3811 hours at 2000 N; the NU205E manages 4619 hours at twice the load. That is the whole argument for a roller bearing, and it costs you the ability to locate the shaft.

Notice also what the machine factor did. Without it, P would be 4000 N, C/P would be 7,33 and the life would be 8481 hours. Entering fw = 1,2 nearly halved the calculated life — a 20 % adjustment to the load, and the exponent turned it into 46 %.

Reading the result

L10h against the machine class, exactly as for a ball bearing: 4000–8000 h intermittent, 20 000–30 000 h daily production, 50 000 h and above for continuous plant.

Static safety against the worst standstill or shock load. Roller bearings have generous static ratings because the contact line spreads the load, so S₀ is rarely the problem — but check it anyway if the machine is stopped under load.

The adjusted life applies the reliability factor a₁ only. The ISO 281:2007 modifier aISO for lubrication, contamination and fatigue load limit is not included. Roller bearings are more sensitive to misalignment than ball bearings, and misalignment is not in any of these numbers — a few minutes of arc can shorten the real life far more than anything on this page.

Frequently asked questions

Why can I not enter an axial load?

Because an NU or N type cannot carry one. If you need combined loading, use a deep groove, angular contact or tapered roller bearing instead.

What does the E suffix mean?

A reinforced internal design with larger rolling elements. Same mounting dimensions, considerably higher rating. Where it exists it is almost always the better choice.

Why is the exponent 10/3 instead of 3?

It comes from the line contact between roller and raceway rather than the point contact of a ball. The value is empirical and is fixed by ISO 281.

Which standard is this?

ISO 281 for rating life, ISO 76 for static load rating.
