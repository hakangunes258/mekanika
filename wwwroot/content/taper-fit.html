How a taper fit is calculated

A taper fit is an interference fit whose interference you set during assembly rather than during machining. Pressing the hub a measured distance up the cone stretches it by a fixed amount, and that amount — not a tolerance band — decides the contact pressure. This is why a taper joint can be taken apart and put back together without losing its grip, and why the same joint can be tuned on the bench.

The taper ratio does the conversion, and it divides: on a 1:10 cone, pushing the hub 0,30 mm along the axis increases the radius by only 0,015 mm — a tenth of the travel on the diameter, a twentieth on the radius. That reduction is the point. It is what makes the interference settable on the bench to a few microns with ordinary shop measurement. From there the calculation is the same thick-cylinder problem as a cylindrical press fit: interference minus roughness loss gives contact pressure, and pressure times friction times area gives the torque the joint can carry.

The equations

The taper converts axial travel into radial interference, and Lamé's thick-cylinder solution converts interference into pressure:

U_r = ds / (2X)                        taper 1:X, radial interference
U_e = 2 U_r - 0,8 (Rz_1 + Rz_2)        effective diametral interference

p   = U_e / [ d_m . (C_1/E_1 + C_2/E_2) ]

T_cap = mu_d . p . pi . d_m^2 . L / 2                    torque capacity
F_asm = p . pi . d_m . L . (mu_s/cos phi + tan phi)      force to press ON
F_ext = p . pi . d_m . L . (mu_s/cos phi - tan phi)      force to pull OFF
F_a   = p . pi . d_m . L . (mu_d/cos phi - tan phi)      axial capacity in service

self-locking  when  mu_s/cos phi > tan phi   (i.e. mu_s > sin phi)

Note which friction coefficient goes where. The static coefficient governs assembly, extraction and self-locking — every one of those is a break-loose condition. The dynamic one governs sliding in service, so it is what carries torque and an axial load.

Self-locking is a property of the friction coefficient, not of the taper ratio. Fext above goes negative as soon as the wedge term beats the friction term, and the cone then pushes its own hub off: it has no axial holding capacity and needs a nut holding at least p·π·dm·L·(tan φ − µs/cos φ). A fixed angle cut-off cannot see this: at µs = 0,10 a 1:5 cone (φ = 5,71°) is already at its limit, while at µs = 0,15 it still holds.

Torque and an axial force draw on the same friction interface. Their two capacities are not equal here, because the wedge opposes an axial load and does nothing to a torque, so they are combined as (Ft/Ft,cap)² + (Fa/Fa,cap)² ≤ 1 rather than as one resultant. With only one load acting this returns that load's own safety factor.

Where the inputs come from

Taper ratio. Pick a standard cone unless you have a reason not to: 1:10 is the usual shaft-end taper, 1:5 is steeper and releases more easily, and Morse tapers around 1:20 are shallow enough to be self-holding. Anything shallower than about 1:20 will not come apart without a puller.

Axial displacement. This is the design variable and the one the fitter actually controls. It is measured from the position where the cones just touch with no force — not from a face or a shoulder. Getting that datum wrong is the most common way a taper joint ends up loose.

Mean diameter. Measured at mid-length of the contact, not at the large end. The calculator derives the small and large diameters from it.

Roughness. Same rule as a cylindrical fit: use the Rz you will machine to. A ground cone at Rz 4 µm keeps interference that a turned one at Rz 16 µm throws away.

Friction. 0,15 static and 0,12 dynamic are reasonable for dry steel on steel. Oil on the cone during assembly lowers both and can drop the joint below its design torque, which is why taper joints are assembled dry.

Worked example

A C45 hub on a C45 shaft end: mean diameter 50 mm, 60 mm of contact, 1:10 taper, hub outside diameter 90 mm, both surfaces Rz 6,3 µm, µs = 0,15, µd = 0,12, pressed on 0,30 mm, carrying 250 Nm.

The joint carries three times the design torque, and the hub is nowhere near yield. The number that actually constrains this design is the 53,5 kN assembly force — that is a hydraulic nut or a press, not a hammer.

Note the pressure along the cone. The diametral interference Δs/X is the same at every section, but the local diameter is not, so with a constant hub outside diameter the pressure rises towards the small end. The hub stress there is 6 % above the mean-diameter value, which is why the results are reported from the governing section rather than from dm.

Steepen the cone to 1:2 (φ = 14,04°, tan φ = 0,250 against µs/cos φ = 0,155) and the joint stops holding itself. The axial capacity is zero, not a large positive number, and a nut has to supply about 179 kN just to keep the hub seated — which the calculator does not model, so the torque capacity shown assumes that preload is present. The same cone also shows what reading the mean diameter alone costs: its small end is Ø35 against Ø65 at the large end, so the local pressure runs 350 MPa rather than the 199 MPa at dm, and the hub safety factor is 0,60 rather than 0,85.

Halve the displacement to 0,15 mm and the picture inverts. Interference after roughness falls to 4,92 µm, pressure to 7,01 MPa, and the torque capacity to 198,1 Nm — a safety factor of 0,79 against the same 250 Nm. Halving the travel did not halve the capacity, it cut it to a quarter. The roughness loss is a fixed subtraction, so it eats a larger and larger share of the interference as the displacement gets smaller.

Reading the result

Safety against sliding is the primary check. Aim for 1,5 or more. Read the resultant row when both a torque and an axial force act. Unlike a cylindrical fit there is no minimum-material case to worry about, because you set the interference at assembly — but that also means an under-pressed joint is entirely a shop-floor error, not a tolerance one.

Self-locking, before anything else. If the cone does not hold itself at the friction coefficient you entered, the axial capacity is zero and the whole result depends on a retaining nut that this calculator does not size for you. The results say so explicitly and give the force the nut has to carry.

Hub yield at the small end, where the diameter is least and the local pressure therefore highest — with a constant hub outside diameter it is not the large end that governs. Keep von Mises below yield with a factor of about 1,5. A hollow shaft is stressed hardest at its own bore rather than at the cone surface; the results name the point each figure was read at.

Assembly force decides what equipment you need. It rises roughly in step with pressure, so doubling the grip doubles the press. Above about 100 kN, oil-injection assembly is usually cheaper than a bigger press.

A taper that is too shallow will not release; one that is too steep does not hold itself at all. 1:10 with a nut is the common compromise.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my joint weaker than the cylindrical fit of the same size?

Because the interference is whatever you pressed on, and a typical 0,2–0,3 mm of travel on a 1:10 cone gives less than an H7/s6 fit does. Taper joints are chosen for serviceability, not for maximum grip.

Do I need a retaining nut?

That depends on the friction coefficient as much as on the taper. The condition is µs/cos φ > tan φ: at µs = 0,15 everything down to about 1:3,5 holds itself, at µs = 0,10 only about 1:5 and shallower. The calculator states whether your cone is self-locking and gives the force a nut would have to supply if it is not. In practice a nut is fitted on most taper joints regardless, to keep the seating from working loose under vibration, and the calculator does not model it — it assumes the displacement is maintained.

Can I use a hollow shaft?

Yes — enter the bore. A hollow shaft is more compliant, so the same displacement produces less pressure, but it is stressed hardest at its bore rather than at the cone surface, where the radial stress has relaxed to zero.

What is not covered?

The retaining nut and its preload. Elastic–plastic design. The pressure peak at the ends of the hub. Any change in interference from temperature or speed — unlike the cylindrical fit calculator, this module has no inputs for either. And the taper tolerances themselves (angle deviation, contact pattern), which in practice decide how much of the cone is actually bearing.

Which standard is this?

The same thick-cylinder basis as DIN 7190, applied to a conical interface, with taper ratios from DIN 254 / ISO 1119.
