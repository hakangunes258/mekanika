How a cylindrical gear pair is calculated

Gear rating is not one calculation but several running in parallel, each describing a different way a tooth can fail. Tooth root breakage is a bending fatigue problem at the fillet. Pitting is a contact fatigue problem on the flank. Scuffing is an instantaneous lubrication failure driven by temperature, and micropitting is a slow surface distress driven by film thickness. They do not share a governing input, and a gear can be comfortable on three and marginal on the fourth.

Almost all of the effort in ISO 6336 goes into the load factors that sit between the nominal tooth force and the stress that is actually rated. KA covers what the driven machine does, KV the dynamic effects of running speed, KHβ the fact that shafts deflect so the load is not even across the face, and KHα the sharing between teeth in mesh. On a typical pair these multiply to something between 2 and 3, so they matter more than the nominal force that produced them.

The equations

The two fatigue criteria, each as a stress compared against a permissible stress:

Fₜ = 2000 P / (ω d₁)               nominal tangential force

σᵛ₀ = Fₜ/(b mₙ) · Yᵮ Yₛ Yᵝ Yᵝ₋ Yᵛ      nominal root stress
σᵛ  = σᵛ₀ · Kₐ Kᵛ Kᵛᵝ Kᵛα           → Sᵛ = σᵛᵧ / σᵛ

σᵗ₀ = Zᵗ Zᵉ Zε Zᵝ √[Fₜ(u+1)/(d₁ b u)]   nominal contact stress
σᵗ  = Zᵝ σᵗ₀ √(Kₐ Kᵛ Kᵗᵝ Kᵗα)      → Sᵗ = σᵗᵧ / σᵗ

Note that the flank stress carries the square root of the load factors, because contact stress goes as the square root of load. The same K factor therefore hurts the root roughly twice as much as the flank.

Where the inputs come from

Accuracy grade. This is the input people underestimate. The grade sets the manufacturing deviations that feed KV, KHβ and KHα, and moving two grades can change the safety factors by more than changing the material. Specify what the shop will actually hold.

Shaft data for KHβ. Shaft deflection, not the gear teeth, is what dominates the face load factor. If you leave the shaft fields blank the calculator stands in a representative shaft and says so in the results; it never treats the shaft as rigid, because that is the one answer guaranteed to be wrong. On the default pair below this produces KHβ = 2,096, and it is the single largest factor in the whole rating.

The lubricant. Pick a real product from the list. The FZG scuffing stage is a property of a specific oil, not of a viscosity grade, so every entry is taken from a manufacturer's published data sheet and a value that is not published is left blank rather than invented.

Life and application factor. KA describes the driven machine's shock, and the required hours set the life factors. Both move the answer substantially and neither can be read off the drawing.

Follow KHβ = 2,096. That single factor more than doubles the load used for rating, and it comes from shaft deflection and manufacturing lead deviation — not from the gear teeth at all. The root stress roughly doubles from 126 to 250 MPa entirely through the K factors, and the flank stress rises by about half because it only carries their square root.

The result is a pair that is comfortable on bending (2,98) and marginal on pitting (1,11) — the normal picture for a hardened gear, where the case supports high bending but the flank is limited by contact fatigue. Micropitting at 1,09 is the weakest link, and it responds to smoother flanks and higher viscosity, not to a stronger steel.

Reading the result

Root safety SF ≥ 1,4 and flank safety SH ≥ 1,0 are the usual minimum targets; industrial practice often asks for 1,5 and 1,2. The two thresholds differ because a root failure breaks the tooth off and a flank failure gives years of warning.

Judge scuffing on the margin in kelvin, not on SB. ISO/TR 13989 says a safety expressed as a ratio of temperatures may cause confusion and suggests a demanded difference of, for instance, 50 K. The results colour the row on the margin for that reason.

Two deliberate conservatisms are named in the results. KHβ stays on the additive Eq. (52) with B = 1, where commercial packages apply the compensating Eq. (53) once told the contact pattern is favourable — worth roughly 9 % on KHβ. And σFlim comes from ISO 6336-5:2016 rather than the 2006 edition. If you are comparing against other software and find this module a few per cent lower, these are the likely reasons.

Out of scope, and stated on the page: tooth flank fracture, planetary and internal arrangements, and within micropitting the profile-modified load sharing branches.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my flank safety so much lower than my root safety?

Because case-hardened steel is very strong in bending and limited on the surface. That split is normal. If you need more flank capacity, more face width or a larger centre distance helps; a higher-grade steel mostly does not.

Why does the accuracy grade change the answer so much?

Because the deviations it defines feed straight into KV, KHβ and KHα, and those factors multiply the rated load. Manufacturing quality is a load question, not just a noise question.

Can I ignore the shaft data?

You can leave it blank, and the calculator substitutes a representative shaft and tells you it has done so. You cannot get a meaningful KHβ without some assumption about the shaft.

Which standard is this?

ISO 6336 parts 1, 2, 3 and 5 for rating; ISO 1328-1:2013 for tolerances; ISO/TR 13989 for scuffing; ISO/TR 15144-1 for micropitting; DIN 3967 for tooth thickness allowances. Note that ISO 6336-1:2006 normatively references the 1995 edition of ISO 1328-1, whose grades are not interchangeable with the 2013 classes — the results say so rather than silently equating them.
